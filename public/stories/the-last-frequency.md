# The Last Frequency

The radio tower had been silent for eleven years when Meera climbed it.

She wasn't supposed to be up here. The rusted ladder had swayed under her weight, groaning like something alive and reluctant. But the city looked different from 200 feet — smaller, softer, the way a wound looks after it scabs over.

Her father had worked this tower. Every morning for thirty years he'd climbed it before sunrise, adjusting antennae, logging signal strength in a green notebook. She still had the notebooks. Thirty of them, stacked by her bed, filled with numbers she didn't understand and margins filled with small drawings of birds.

She pressed her palm against the cold metal and tried to feel something.

---

The last entry in his final notebook wasn't numbers.

*The funny thing about signals,* he'd written, *is that they don't disappear. They just travel further out. Everything you send keeps going — past the atmosphere, past the moon, past wherever our instruments can follow. Somewhere out there, the first thing I ever broadcast is still moving.*

She'd read it a hundred times. She still wasn't sure if it was about radio waves.

---

The city below her flickered on. One light, then a cluster, then whole grids of orange and white, like a slow conversation starting up.

She thought about frequencies. About how her voice, her father's voice, the static between them — none of it had stopped. It had just moved somewhere she couldn't tune to yet.

She started climbing down.

The tower groaned.

She decided to take it as a goodbye.
